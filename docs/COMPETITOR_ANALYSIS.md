# Competitor Analysis — Brain Battle

Part B concept-innovation evidence. Every factual claim about another product is
sourced; see **References** at the end. Where a source could not confirm
something, that is stated rather than filled in.

**Brain Battle is not the first multiplayer quiz game.** Quiz games are a large,
long-established category. What follows argues a difference in *interaction
model*, not in novelty of the genre.

---

## Summary

| | Kahoot! | Trivia Crack | Jackbox Party Pack | **Brain Battle** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary setting | Classroom / workplace | Personal mobile | Living room | **Living room** |
| Shared screen | Yes (host display) | No | Yes (TV) | **Yes (TV)** |
| Devices per player | One each | One each | One each (phone) | **None — one shared remote** |
| Network required | Yes | Yes | Yes | **No** |
| Account required | To create | Yes | No (to join) | **No** |
| Content | User-generated | Publisher-authored | Publisher-authored | Publisher-authored |

## 1. Kahoot!

### Product purpose
A Norwegian game-based learning platform. Its games, called "kahoots", are
user-generated multiple-choice quizzes accessed through a web browser or the
Kahoot! app. It launched publicly in December 2012, with origins in a series of
"Lecture Quiz" prototypes developed at NTNU from 2006. [1]

### Target audience
Wikipedia does not state a target audience directly, but its coverage centres on
classrooms and students, and it notes corporate training through the Actimo and
Motimate acquisitions. [1] The platform is therefore best understood as
education-first, with a secondary workplace-training market.

### Typical interaction model
Host-led and multi-device: a host runs a live session on a shared display while
participants answer on their own devices, joining through the game entry point
`kahoot.it`. Registration is optional for taking part in a quiz and required for
creating one. [1][2]

### Scale (for context, not comparison)
Kahoot! reported one billion cumulative participating players in March 2017, and
Wikipedia's infobox lists 12 billion non-unique players as of 2025. The company
was acquired in 2023 by a group of investors including Goldman Sachs Asset
Management for $1.72 billion in cash, and delisted from the Oslo Stock Exchange
in January 2024. [1][3]

These figures are cited to show the **category** is large and commercially
serious. They are not a forecast for Brain Battle.

### Similarities with Brain Battle
- Multiple-choice questions with a timer and a points-based score
- A large shared screen that everyone watches
- Turn structure and a leaderboard as the social payoff

### Differences from Brain Battle
- **Every participant needs their own device.** Brain Battle needs none.
- **It requires a network.** Brain Battle makes no network request at all.
- **It is pedagogical first.** Brain Battle is entertainment first.
- **Content is user-generated**, which is its core strength. Brain Battle ships a
  fixed authored bank.
- It runs in a browser or app, not as a native television application.

### Opportunities to differentiate Brain Battle
Kahoot!'s model assumes a room where everyone has a phone and a working network.
A living room at home frequently fails both assumptions — a guest's phone is
flat, the Wi-Fi is slow, nobody wants to join a session. Brain Battle's
zero-device, zero-network model removes the entire setup step that Kahoot!
depends on.

## 2. Trivia Crack

### Product purpose
A free mobile trivia game by Etermax (director Maximo Cavazzani), released on
Android on 26 October 2013 and iOS on 6 November 2013, originally in Argentina
as *Preguntados*. [4]

### Target audience
Mass-market mobile players. Wikipedia reports more than 600 million downloads
worldwide and more than 150 million active users annually. [4]

### Typical interaction model
Competitive question-answering across six categories, each represented by a
character: Tito (Geography), Albert (Science), Bonzo (Sports), Hector (History),
Pop (Entertainment) and Tina (Art), plus Andy, a crown allowing a free category
choice. [4] Contemporary coverage describes a spinning category wheel and
turn-based one-versus-one play in which correct answers earn another spin and
collecting all six characters wins. [5]

*Note: the Wikipedia article does not describe the wheel mechanic or the
monetisation model, so those rest on the secondary source.* Secondary coverage
describes a free, advertising-supported model with purchasable coins for perks
such as skipping a question. [5]

It is available on iOS, Android, Amazon Fire OS, Windows Phone, Facebook, Google
Assistant, Amazon Alexa, Twitch, Apple Watch and CrazyGames. [4] **Apple TV is
not among them.**

### Similarities with Brain Battle
- Category-based multiple-choice trivia as the core loop
- Six broad categories against Brain Battle's five
- Turn-based competition between players

### Differences from Brain Battle
- **Personal and often asynchronous.** Players need not be in the same room, or
  playing at the same time. Brain Battle requires everyone physically present.
- **One screen per player.** Brain Battle is one screen for everybody.
- **Monetised through advertising and consumable purchases.** Brain Battle's
  prototype contains no advertising and no purchases of any kind.
- Not available on Apple TV.

### Opportunities to differentiate Brain Battle
Trivia Crack is a game you play *at* other people, usually alone on a sofa with
a phone. Brain Battle is a game you play *with* the people in the room. The
differentiator is the social setting, not the question format — and the absence
of advertising and consumable purchases is a meaningful quality difference for a
family audience.

## 3. The Jackbox Party Pack

**The closest structural competitor, and the one already on Apple TV.**

### Product purpose
A series of party-game collections designed for a group in one room around a
shared screen. [6]

### Target audience
Groups of friends and families in a living room; also used for remote play over
video calls. [6][7]

### Typical interaction model
The host device runs the game on a TV and displays a room code. Other players
open `jackbox.tv` in a phone browser and enter the code — no installation
required, and only one copy of the game is needed for the whole group. Private
answers on personal devices, revealed on the shared screen, are central to the
design. An internet connection is required during play. [6][7][8]

It is available on a wide range of platforms **including Apple TV**. [6]

### Similarities with Brain Battle
- Shared television screen as the primary display
- Local, same-room, social play
- One purchase covers the whole group
- **Both ship on Apple TV**

### Differences from Brain Battle
- **Every player still needs a phone and a browser.** Brain Battle needs only the
  remote already in the room.
- **It requires an internet connection during play.** Brain Battle requires none.
- Jackbox is a varied party-game collection; Brain Battle is a focused trivia game.
- Jackbox's content leans adult and comedic; Brain Battle's bank is
  general-knowledge and family-appropriate.

### Opportunities to differentiate Brain Battle
Jackbox proves the living-room shared-screen model works commercially on Apple
TV. Brain Battle's differentiation is removing the last two dependencies Jackbox
keeps — **the phones and the network**. That matters for a family with young
children who have no phones, for guests who will not join a web session, and
anywhere connectivity is unreliable. It is a narrower product, but it starts
instantly.

## Where Brain Battle actually sits

The honest positioning is not "better than" these products. It is a different
point on one axis: **how much setup a group must do before the first question**.

```
                 Setup required before play
  none ──────────────────────────────────────────────> significant

  Brain Battle        Jackbox            Kahoot!
  (remote only)       (phones +          (accounts, host
                       network)           session, devices)
```

Every product to the right buys capability with setup cost — user-generated
content, private answers, remote play. Brain Battle trades all of that away for
one property: **a group can start playing in under a minute with nothing but the
Apple TV remote.**

### Where the gap is real

- Trivia Crack is **not on Apple TV** at all. [4]
- Jackbox **is** on Apple TV but still needs phones and a network. [6][7]
- Kahoot! is browser/app-based and education-first. [1]

None of this makes Brain Battle unique as a quiz game. It does make the
*no-second-device, no-network* trivia experience on Apple TV a position the
researched competitors do not occupy.

### Honest competitive weaknesses

- **No user-generated content.** Kahoot!'s greatest strength is one Brain Battle
  does not have at all.
- **No remote play.** Jackbox and Trivia Crack both support players who are not
  in the room.
- **A far smaller question bank** — 50 questions against commercial libraries.
- **No private answers.** With one shared screen and one remote, everyone sees
  everything. Jackbox's phone-based privacy enables bluffing games Brain Battle
  cannot do.
- **Turn-based, not simultaneous.** Only one person plays at a time, so a
  four-player match has more watching than answering.
